## 0. Executive summary — what matters most to Luna Academy

The strongest CSA material for this project falls into six practical themes:

1. **Food production:** a lunar greenhouse needs controlled light, water, gases, temperature and shielding. Lunar regolith is not ready-to-use agricultural soil; hydroponic or aeroponic systems are suggested as possible approaches.
2. **Daily life:** food, hydration, hygiene, waste, sleep, exercise, communications and leisure all require deliberate systems in a closed habitat.
3. **Crew wellbeing:** isolation, stress, fatigue, sleep disruption and limited social contact are real design concerns for long missions.
4. **Engineering:** children can learn through a design loop — define a problem, identify constraints, brainstorm, prototype, test, improve and explain.
5. **Surface work:** rover driving, sample collection, lunar geology, resupply and rescue provide practical activities with maths, navigation, science and teamwork.
6. **Robotics:** robotic arms and autonomous systems inspire maintenance and construction interactions, but some CSA examples (such as Canadarm3) are Gateway/orbital systems, not surface-base machines.

## 3. Life support and day-to-day living

### 3.1 Food and nutrition

CSA material says food for space missions must be compact, light, nutritious, tasty, shelf-stable and designed to minimize crumbs or particles. On the ISS, the standard menu includes three meals and one snack; the CSA page gives a range of **1,900–3,200 kcal per day**, depending on an astronaut's needs. This is an ISS reference range, not a fixed prescription for every lunar-colony person.

**Game systems to implement:**

- Food inventory with expiry/shelf-life and storage conditions.
- Balanced meal planning and crew preferences.
- Food preparation and waste management.
- Greenhouse harvest feeding into the kitchen inventory.
- A food-safety inspection event if storage, handling or hygiene is poor.
- Social meals as an NPC relationship and morale activity.

**Educational outcomes:** nutrition, reading quantities, comparing food choices, planning supplies, food safety and respecting other people's preferences.

### 3.2 Growing food on the Moon

CSA's *Interplanetary Farming* activity identifies several lunar growing constraints:

- Liquid water is not readily available at the surface; polar regions may offer access to water resources, but a habitat would still need a supply and processing system.
- Light is a major design consideration. The activity describes an approximately 28-Earth-day lunar day at the equator, with roughly 14 Earth days of daylight and 14 of darkness; it notes near-continuous sunlight at selected South Pole locations. Do not interpret this as every polar site receiving perfect uninterrupted sunlight.
- Plants need controlled growing conditions and protection from radiation.
- The Moon has no breathable atmosphere for plants; the greenhouse must provide the required gases and regulate temperature.
- Lunar regolith does not contain the nutrients needed for ordinary crop growth; hydroponic or aeroponic systems are suggested.

**CSA crop examples (illustrative growing-time estimates from the activity):**

| Crop | Approximate growth time | Example learning/game use |
|---|---:|---|
| Green-leaf lettuce | 21–28 days | Fast harvest, salads, water/light tuning |
| Soybean | 45–65 days | Protein and nutrition; needs processing |
| Carrot | 70–80 days | Longer planning horizon and storage |

Crop choice should consider edible biomass, nutritional value, ease of growth, size, flavour, pollination, and light/water/nutrient/temperature/humidity needs. A “pick-and-eat” crop is useful when food-processing equipment is limited.

**Gameplay:** Amina asks the player to choose a crop, allocate light and water, check sensors and build a small pollination mechanism. The player can inspect growth, diagnose a problem and improve the greenhouse without being forced into a linear mission.

### 3.3 Water, hygiene and waste

The CSA *Personal hygiene in space* page explains that water behaves differently in microgravity, so ordinary showers and handwashing do not work as they do on Earth. Astronauts use washcloths and no-rinse cleaning products; the page also describes vacuum-assisted toilet equipment and wastewater recycling on the ISS. It cites an ISS water-recovery capability of up to 93% in the linked educational video context.

**How to use this in the Moon game:**

- Model a clean-water tank, wastewater tank, filters, pumps and water-quality sensors.
- Make leaks, clogged filters and contamination optional incidents.
- Let the NPC crew follow hygiene routines.
- Make the toilet a serviceable closed-loop system, not a magical invisible bathroom.
- Track the difference between water recovered, water lost, water stored and water allocated.

**Accuracy note:** the 93% figure is an ISS reference. Do not hard-code it as a guaranteed Moon-base performance value; make Luna Academy's system parameters explicitly fictional or configurable unless supported by a mission design source.

### 3.4 Exercise, sleep and recreation

CSA's *Living aboard Orion* describes a small spacecraft crew planning meals, hygiene, exercise, communication, sleep and leisure. The Orion page describes a 30-minute workout nearly every day for the short Artemis II capsule mission and eight hours of scheduled sleep. CSA's broader ISS exercise material describes about two hours per day of exercise during long-duration microgravity missions.

These are **different vehicles and mission contexts**. Orion is a compact, short-duration capsule; the ISS is a long-duration microgravity laboratory. Neither should be copied as a universal exercise prescription for people living on the lunar surface.

**Gameplay systems:**

- Crew daily schedule and sleep quality.
- Exercise room and fitness equipment maintenance.
- Food variety and shared meals.
- Leisure options: music, books, games, windows/observation dome and personal hobbies.
- A wellness panel that helps the player notice patterns, not diagnose illness.

### 3.5 Mental health and isolation

CSA describes possible effects of isolation and demanding space work, including stress, anxiety, fatigue, sleep problems, irritability, mood changes, boredom and emotional distress. Limited social contact and distance from family/friends can increase isolation. Purposeful work, positive crew relationships, training and support are important.

**Use in NPC storytelling:**

- A character may be quiet after a difficult shift, miss a family message or feel frustrated by a long repair.
- The player can listen, help organize a team activity, share a meal, or reduce workload after a stressful event.
- NPCs should not be reduced to a “morale bar.” Use respectful dialogue and small personal story moments.
- Never punish children for not being a therapist. Offer supportive choices and model asking for qualified help when needed.

## 4. Moon exploration, geology and rover work

### 4.1 Explore and study the lunar surface

CSA's *Learning about the Moon* activity uses WorldWide Telescope to help youth explore the Moon, its motion, lunar phases and eclipses. *My Moon* encourages searching for interesting places and learning lunar geography and Apollo history.

**Game translation:**

- A 3D surface map with named points of interest.
- A science journal where the player records a location, photo, sample and observation.
- A simple map/compass/coordinate interaction.
- Optional observatory interactions that explain phases and eclipses without turning the whole game into a quiz.

### 4.2 Craters and impact science

CSA's crater activity asks learners to study how crater size, ejecta and shape depend on the incoming object's mass, velocity, angle and height. It explains that lunar craters remain visible for a very long time because the Moon does not have Earth's weathering processes and wind-driven landscape change.

**Game translation:**

- Let the player compare two crater sites and inspect their rims, slopes and ejecta.
- Amina or another NPC explains what can be inferred from the shape.
- Use a small safe experiment UI to change mass, speed and impact angle and observe a simplified result.
- Do not pretend that a child can calculate exact lunar impact physics from a toy formula; label it as a learning model.

### 4.3 Rover exploration and samples

The CSA rover toolkit links to a Canadian lunar rover concept, rover payloads and a lunar rover mini-game. The CSA page titled *First Canadian rover to explore the Moon* describes a South Pole rover concept intended to investigate a region, gather science data, study geology and help search for water ice. **Important status note:** the linked page currently labels that particular project “Project ended.” Do not present that named project as an active future mission.

**Game translation:**

- Rover garage: inspect wheels, battery, cameras, sensors and sample tools.
- Route planning: choose a route using slope, obstacles, distance and communication coverage.
- Field work: identify a rock, take a measurement, collect a sample and label it.
- Return: unload samples, recharge and report findings.
- A rover can be autonomous for a simple task but should still need human oversight and maintenance.

## 5. Engineering, robotics and building

### 5.1 Engineering design loop

CSA's simple pollination activity teaches a repeatable design process:

1. Define the problem.
2. Identify constraints (time, money, materials) and success criteria.
3. Brainstorm several solutions.
4. Choose a promising design.
5. Prototype.
6. Test and evaluate.
7. Improve the design.
8. Communicate the solution.

This is an excellent foundation for Luna Academy's base-building system.

### 5.2 Pollination mechanics as a mini-build

CSA's *A simple pollination system* and *Pollinating pulleys* ask learners to design a mechanism to transfer pollen between plants. They explore pulleys, wheels and axles, materials, prototypes and testing. The source explains that some plants rely on cross-pollination and that controlled space environments may require a device or other method to transfer pollen.

**Gameplay feature:** allow players to place a track, pulley, small actuator or robotic arm in the greenhouse, test whether it reaches two plants, then improve alignment and reliability. This is a practical construction puzzle tied to biology.

### 5.3 Construction and resupply

The CSA *Lunar Resupply Mission* activity asks learners to design, budget, build and test a lander that safely delivers a payload to the Moon. That provides a strong game mechanic for both new buildings and logistics.

**Proposed Luna Academy building loop (inspired by planning/building games, but scientifically adapted):**

- Choose a valid site and inspect terrain.
- Select a building blueprint.
- Check footprint, slope, access, power, water, thermal needs and nearby services.
- Set a construction budget and reserve materials.
- Build in visible stages using NPC crew and robotic equipment.
- Connect utilities and run a commissioning test.
- Operate, maintain and upgrade the building.

Buildings should have functions and dependencies, not merely decorative “levels.” An upgrade should change real capability: extra storage, a better filter, more greenhouse beds, stronger shielding, a more efficient pump or an additional workbench.

### 5.4 Robotics and Canadarm3

CSA describes Canadarm3 as a robotic system for Gateway. It is useful inspiration for autonomous inspection, manipulation and maintenance around a space station. It is **not a lunar surface crane** and should not be depicted as a system already operating at a Moon base.

For Luna Academy, use distinct robotics roles:

- A small utility robot for transport and inspection.
- A rover for terrain travel and sample work.
- A fixed robotic arm for workshop or greenhouse tasks.
- A human NPC who plans, supervises, repairs and checks autonomous systems.

## 8. Six sample sandbox events grounded in CSA educational themes

### Event 1 — Greenhouse pollination rail

- **Trigger:** Amina reports low fruit set in one crop section.
- **Player choice:** Inspect plants, review pollen transfer, build or repair a pollination mechanism, or ask Amina for a hint.
- **Learning:** Plant reproduction, simple machines, prototyping and testing.
- **No forced failure:** If the player leaves, the plants continue their simulated growth state and the event remains available.

### Event 2 — Lunar sample return

- **Trigger:** A rover finds a scientifically interesting rock near a crater rim.
- **Player choice:** Plan the route, check rover battery, choose a sample tool, collect and label the sample, then return it to the lab.
- **Learning:** Geology, measurement, navigation, energy planning and scientific records.

### Event 3 — Supply lander construction

- **Trigger:** A supply shipment is planned, but payload mass and available materials are limited.
- **Player choice:** Choose which items to send, design the container/lander configuration, balance budget and mass, and run a delivery test.
- **Learning:** Budgeting, ratios, engineering constraints, payload prioritization and testing.

### Event 4 — Crew member rescue

- **Trigger:** A human NPC is delayed outside the habitat after a rover issue.
- **Player choice:** Gather the correct rescue equipment, communicate with the NPC, plan the route and bring them back safely.
- **Learning:** Teamwork, empathy, planning, safety and resource use.

### Event 5 — Food contamination check

- **Trigger:** A sensor or crew report flags a storage container or greenhouse harvest for inspection.
- **Player choice:** Trace the food path from growing/harvest to storage and preparation, inspect labels and isolate suspect stock.
- **Learning:** Microbiology basics, hygiene, food chains and evidence-based decisions.

### Event 6 — Isolated and overworked NPC

- **Trigger:** A long period of work, poor sleep or low social interaction affects an NPC's wellbeing state.
- **Player choice:** Listen, arrange a shared meal or recreation activity, redistribute a task with Maya, or encourage the NPC to speak with Sofia.
- **Learning:** Mental health awareness, empathy, teamwork and work/rest balance.

## 9. Learning architecture for ages 7–13 — without age gates

The CSA offers resources spanning Kindergarten to Grade 12, with many relevant activities around Grades 3–9. Luna Academy should use **adaptive depth**, not age selection.

| Adaptive layer | What changes | Example: rover route |
|---|---|---|
| Discover | Clear visual cues and simple language | Follow a visible safe path around a rock |
| Understand | Short explanation and one variable at a time | Learn what slope and battery range mean |
| Experiment | Compare alternatives | Choose between a short steep path and a longer flat route |
| Apply | Combine several systems | Balance distance, slope, battery, sample mass and communication |
| Explain | Reflect on why a choice worked | Record a short reason in the science journal |

The player can request hints at any time. A child who already understands a topic can try a deeper challenge. Do not lock the world, NPCs or stations behind age bands or linear levels.

## 11. Visual and audio direction — research-informed, not a CSA art specification

- **Environment:** restrained lunar palette, long shadows, high contrast between sunlit ridges and permanently shadowed terrain, with clear habitat lighting cues.
- **Habitat interiors:** practical modules, clear equipment labels, status lamps, service panels, storage locations and visible utility routes.
- **Greenhouse:** healthy plant colours and visible growing beds, lighting, water lines, fans and pollination equipment.
- **Rover:** wheels, sensor mast, camera, sample container, manipulator and charging connection should be visually readable.
- **NPC storytelling:** facial expression, posture, voice and small daily routines carry emotional meaning; do not make every human character look like a generic quest marker.
- **Audio:** suit/airlock sounds, fans, pumps, low equipment hum, rover motors, soft habitat ambience and clear non-alarming notification tones.
- **Accessibility:** subtitles, adjustable text size, clear icons, low-motion UI options, remappable controls and non-timed alternatives for difficult tasks.

## 12. Accuracy guardrails for the design team

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Use CSA materials as educational references and inspiration | Treat every educational activity as a validated lunar operating procedure |
| Mark ISS and Orion examples as microgravity | Copy their physics directly to the lunar surface |
| Explain that the greenhouse is a controlled environment | Plant ordinary crops directly in raw lunar regolith as if it were garden soil |
| Distinguish Gateway robotics from surface equipment | Put Canadarm3 on the lunar ground and call it an operating surface crane |
| Treat a rover or rescue incident as a simulation | Present a game inventory ranking as real astronaut emergency guidance |
| Give NPCs nuanced emotional lives and support networks | Reduce mental health to a simplistic morale meter or blame the player |
| Use grade labels as resource metadata | Add age-selection screens or age-locked world zones |

## 15. Final recommendation

Use this CSA collection as the **education-and-gameplay reference layer** for Luna Academy. Build one persistent Moon settlement in which the player can freely explore, construct, maintain systems, drive rovers, learn from a human NPC crew and choose optional real-world-inspired situations.

The child should learn by doing: grow food, test a mechanism, inspect a sample, plan a resupply, help a crew member, or repair a system. Each activity should leave the child with a clear connection between **what happened on the Moon** and **the science or life skill they can use on Earth**.
